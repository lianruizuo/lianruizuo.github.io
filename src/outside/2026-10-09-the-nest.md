---
title: The nest on our front door
date: 2026-10-09
place: Nashville
summary: A spring spent watching a pair of birds raise a family in the wreath on our front door.
---

It began quietly.

On March 13th, we noticed the first sign — a small bird placing a twig into the wreath on our front door. At that time, it felt like a curiosity, something gentle and almost accidental. We didn’t know yet that this small act would unfold into a full story — one that would stay with us.

Days passed, and the nest grew slowly. Not in a hurry, not with certainty. There were moments when nothing seemed to change, when the birds disappeared, and we wondered if they had given up. But then they returned, bringing different materials each day: grass, fibers, even cherry blossom petals. It felt like watching patience take form.

On March 27th, the first egg appeared.

That moment shifted everything. What had been observation became anticipation. What had been curiosity became attachment.

Then came uncertainty. The temperature in Nashville dropped below freezing. For two days, the nest was empty. The single egg sat exposed to the cold, and we quietly wondered if the story had ended before it truly began.

But it hadn’t.

The birds returned. They continued. One egg became two, then three, then five. Even after interruption, even after uncertainty, life resumed its course. Looking back, that was perhaps the first lesson — persistence is not loud, but it is powerful.

{% figure "/outside/nest/eggs.jpg", "Five eggs.", "Five pale blue speckled eggs in a nest of dry grass, under the leaves of the wreath." %}

On April 12th, the first two chicks hatched. By April 14th, all five had arrived.

{% figure "/outside/nest/hatching.jpg", "April 12: the first two chicks, beside three eggs.", "Two newly hatched chicks beside three unhatched eggs in the nest." %}

Those days were filled with quiet joy. Each morning brought visible change. They grew so quickly that it felt almost unreal — fragile forms becoming something more defined, more alive. We adjusted our lives around them. We stopped using the front door. We turned off the porch light. We installed a camera. We refilled the feeder. We stopped online shopping. Without realizing it, we had crossed a line — from observers to participants.

{% figure "/outside/nest/chicks.jpg", "Growing by the day.", "Several downy chicks huddled together deep in the nest." %}

We cared.

And then, on April 23rd at 9:31 PM, everything changed.

A notification from the camera — something routine, we thought. But when we opened the app, we saw a snake coiled around the wreath. In that moment, we watched as it took one of the birds. Shock replaced calm. We acted quickly, almost instinctively, driving the snake away. But it was already too late for some of them.

{% figure "/outside/nest/snake.jpg", "That night.", "A dark snake coiled on the ground by the front step at night." %}

That night was heavy. It revealed something we had not fully accepted before: life does not unfold gently just because we wish it to.

We had always said we wanted to observe nature. But in that moment, we were no longer detached. We were part of nature. We were affected.

The next morning, we waited.

Would the parents return? Would the remaining chicks survive?

They did return.

Not everything was lost.

Two were killed that night, and one injured chick did not make it, leaving two. The following days carried a different kind of tension — quieter, but no less real. Each night brought concern. Each morning brought relief. At one point, we made another difficult decision, removing a dead chick from the nest to protect the others. Even that act felt like stepping across a boundary we had once promised not to cross.

But perhaps what we were learning was not how to avoid involvement, but how to hold it responsibly.

And then, this morning, the story shifted again.

At 7:12 AM, I took a photo of the two remaining birds. They stood upright now, feathers formed, alert, alive. Something in their posture felt different — ready.

{% figure "/outside/nest/fledglings.jpg", "7:12 AM, the last morning.", "Two feathered young birds sitting upright at the edge of the nest." %}

By 9:30 AM, one had left.

Fledging had begun.

We heard the parent birds calling, persistently, as if encouraging something unseen. About fifteen minutes later, the second bird followed.

And just like that, the nest was empty.

Not in loss, but in completion.

Looking back, this was never just about birds.

It was about witnessing something real.

Life is ephemeral, which makes it precious.\
Hope is persistence, which makes it resilient.\
Love endures vulnerability, which makes it meaningful.

We started this journey wanting to observe.\
We ended it having experienced something deeper.

We saw that life does not promise smoothness.\
We saw that loss is not the opposite of life — it is part of it.\
We saw that even after disruption, something continues.

And perhaps most importantly:

We learned that to care is to risk pain,\
but also to open the door to meaning.

The wreath will be removed. The door will be used again. Life will return to its usual rhythm.

But something has changed quietly.

This place is no longer just a front door.

It is a place where life once began, struggled, persisted, and moved on.

And we were there to see it.
